Commissions

Likeness, Gravity, and a Pulse.

I accept a limited number of portrait commissions in acrylic. The aim is a work with believable structure, clear presence, disciplined color, and enough painterly life to keep the result from feeling embalmed.

Standard of Execution
A recent self-portrait in acrylic. This demonstrates the level of finish, structural integrity, and directness clients can expect from a commissioned work.

What Clients Can Expect

I care about the bone structure, the asymmetries, the value relationships, and the small, deliberate decisions that make a human face read as a particular person rather than a generic template.

In plain English: the eyes need to hold, the head needs to feel built rather than pasted together, and the painting needs to look alive from ten feet across the room and from ten inches away.

A good portrait should not just say, "Yes, that looks like them." It should say, "There they are."

Whether you are commissioning a self-portrait, a memorial piece, or a visual anchor for a professional space, I deliver work that feels personal without ever collapsing into cheap sentimentality.

First Commission

My first commissioned portrait is a memorial: a woman in a red suit and gold hoop earrings, painted against a vermilion ground with a soft golden light gathering behind her head. The color carries the occasion, warm and steady and a little ceremonial, so the face can stay a face rather than becoming a monument.

It is acrylic on canvas, sealed with Liquitex matte varnish, which protects the paint and removes the glare. The portrait reads the same from the doorway as it does from the chair beneath it.

The Byzantine Method

My current practice is to paint portraits in the Byzantine manner, the way icon painters have built a face for centuries: from dark to light, in thin layers, with the form emerging through light rather than being carved out of shadow. The face begins as a deliberately dark underpainting. Lighter flesh tones are laid over it in successive layers, each lighter than the one before, and the final highlights go on last. The tradition worked in egg tempera on gessoed panel. I work in acrylic, and I will not pretend the two are the same. What carries over is the logic: translucent layers, a face that seems lit from within rather than from the side, and no fussing over detail until the structure underneath is sound.

For a client, that means two practical things. A portrait built in layers cannot be rushed, which is why I ask for the real deadline up front. And the layering is where the depth comes from: the dark underpainting gives the face its weight, and the lights placed over it give it life.

The Rules of Engagement

To ensure the final piece lives up to both of our expectations, I require a few things straight out of the gate.

Reference Photos

Clear, well-lit images are non-negotiable. The better the references, the better the portrait. Send your options, and I will tell you plainly which ones are actually useful.

Size & Setting

Tell me where the piece will live and the rough dimensions. A portrait meant to anchor a corporate boardroom requires a different approach than one resting on a mantle.

Timeline & Budget

Give me the real deadline and the real budget range up front. As I have said before, this saves both of us from pretending miracles are a scheduling strategy.

Preferred Mood

More restrained realism? More painterly interpretation? Severe and direct? Warmer and quieter? State your preference, and we build accordingly.

Begin the Commission

Common formats include 11×14, 12×16, and 16×20 on cradled panel or canvas. If you have your reference photos and details ready, let us begin the conversation.
